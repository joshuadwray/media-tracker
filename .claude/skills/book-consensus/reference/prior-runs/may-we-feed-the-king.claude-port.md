## 1. Where the book stands

Perry's debut novel was published in the UK by Granta on 29 January 2026 (£14.99) and in North America by Transit Books on 29 September 2026 (paperback, 288pp, $18.95, ISBN 9798893380620) ([Books of Brilliance](https://booksofbrilliance.com/2026/09/23/may-we-feed-the-king-rebecca-perry-booker-shortlist/); [Transit Books](https://www.transitbooks.org/books/maywefeedtheking); [Judith Wilson](https://www.judith-wilson.co.uk/journal/book-review-may-we-feed-the-king-by-rebecca-perry-granta-1499)). Perry came to it as an award-winning poet: the collections *Beauty / Beauty* (Bloodaxe, 2015) and *Stone Fruit* (2021), the pamphlets *little armoured* (Seren, 2011) and *cleanliness of rooms and walls* (2017), and the non-fiction *On Trampolining* (Makina Books, 2023); she was shortlisted for the 2016 T. S. Eliot Prize and won the 2017 Michael Murphy Memorial Poetry Prize ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebecca_Perry)). The novel won the 2026 Waterstones Debut Fiction Prize.

## 2. The consensus

Among the reviews I could actually read, the agreement is that the novel's refusals are the point: it withholds the king's name, his period, and direct access to his interiority, and critics read that withholding as method rather than evasion. Annie Hayter in *Big Issue* locates the book's interest in "whether we can trust historical texts and records"; Eric Karl Anderson reads the same gaps as the mechanism by which the king becomes legible at all. The reservation, where it appears, is about momentum and period-feel rather than execution — Judith Wilson grants outright that "you don't know where the plot is going" and argues the voice carries it anyway. No fetched review dissents on quality.

## 4. In-depth readings

**Eric Karl Anderson, Lonesome Reader.** The only review I could read in full that argues a reading rather than reaching a verdict. His claim is that characterisation here runs through obstruction: "I think it's very clever how Rebecca Perry builds such a strong sense of this king's identity without ever giving us direct access to his thoughts or feelings." He extends it past the medieval plot into a general point about being looked at — "All of us are scrutinized, judged, and assumptions are made about us in ways we can't fully control, especially once we become any kind of public figure" — which makes the curator's tableaux and the tourists' gaze one continuous apparatus rather than a framing device.

## 5. Fault lines

No fetched review dissents on the book's quality, so the disagreement sits at the level of what kind of pleasure it offers. Wilson concedes the plot's drift and argues voice compensates. Bayliss's visible opening frames the method as deliberately partial rather than obscure, but his full assessment is paywalled. The one substantive reservation I found anywhere — that the characters' sensibilities feel modern and the book "reads more like a dream or a fairytale than a depiction of a particular period" — appeared only in a search-result summary and is recorded unverified below. Note that the critical tier most likely to carry real dissent (Guardian, Observer, TLS, FT) is the tier I could not reach.

## 8. Synthesis

*My own connection, not a critic's:* every fetched source reaches for a metaphor of mediated looking — keyhole, séance, tableau, tourist — which suggests the book has successfully trained its readers to describe it in its own terms. That's worth noticing as a possible limit on the consensus: critics may be paraphrasing the novel's self-description rather than testing it.
