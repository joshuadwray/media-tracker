# Critical Consensus: *May We Feed the King* by Rebecca Perry (2026)

Compiled 30 September 2026. Every source below was fetched during this run.

## 1. Where the book stands

*May We Feed the King* is Rebecca Perry's debut novel, published in the UK by Granta on 29 January 2026 (hardback, £14.99, ISBN 9781803513867) — [Booker Prizes book page](https://thebookerprizes.com/the-booker-library/books/may-we-feed-the-king); [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/culture/books/review/2026/02/07/may-we-feed-the-king-by-rebecca-perry-a-macabre-fable-about-appetite-in-all-its-guises/). It was longlisted and then shortlisted for the Booker Prize 2026 — [longlist press release](https://thebookerprizes.com/media-centre/press-releases/longlist-for-booker-prize-2026-rewards-risk); [book page](https://thebookerprizes.com/the-booker-library/books/may-we-feed-the-king). It won the Waterstones Debut Fiction Prize 2026 and Perry was named an Observer best debut novelist of 2026 — [Waterstones blog](https://waterstones.com/blog/the-waterstones-podcast-rebecca-perry-wdfp); [Observer review](https://observer.co.uk/culture/books/article/a-feast-for-an-unfit-king). The US edition is published by Transit Books (29 September 2026) — [Transit Books](https://www.transitbooks.org/books/maywefeedtheking); [Linden Tree Books](https://www.lindentreebooks.com/may-we-feed-the-king.html). Perry is a poet: her first collection *Beauty/Beauty* won the Michael Murphy Memorial Prize and was shortlisted for the T.S. Eliot Prize; she also published the nonfiction *On Trampolining* (2023) — [Booker longlist press release](https://thebookerprizes.com/media-centre/press-releases/longlist-for-booker-prize-2026-rewards-risk); [Booker reading guide](https://thebookerprizes.com/the-booker-library/features/reading-guide-may-we-feed-the-king-by-rebecca-perry).

## 2. The consensus

Critics agree this is a formally controlled, poet-written debut whose real subject is not the medieval court but the impossibility of knowing the past. Melissa Harrison (Guardian) calls it "a highly wrought puzzle-box" that "deliberately wrongfoots the reader"; Miriam Balanescu (Irish Times) calls it "a macabre fable about appetite in all its guises" whose project is "to question the fickle nature of storytelling itself"; Olivia Ovenden (Observer) says what it has to say about whether we can trust historical records "is far more interesting" than its lyricism. The main reservation, voiced by Balanescu ("sometimes opts for style over substance") and Johanna Thomas-Corr in the Sunday Times (paywalled — quoted via the [Booker page](https://thebookerprizes.com/the-booker-library/books/may-we-feed-the-king)) ("the sensibilities of the characters feel modern... more like a dream or a fairytale than a depiction of a particular period"), concerns distance and withholding, not execution.

## 4. In-depth readings

**Melissa Harrison, The Guardian.** Harrison's reading centres on the book's epistemology: its "insistence upon detail, its utter specificity, set against a deliberate lack of specificity regarding the larger details." The King's tragedy is "that of someone raised as a subject who has objecthood forced upon him" — ultimate power whose "exercise requires total personal surrender." She ends on the Curator's closing instruction — "Listen for everything and, above all, pay attention" — wondering whether her two close readings were sufficient, which is the review's own verdict: the book rewards exactly the attention it demands.

**Miriam Balanescu, The Irish Times.** Balanescu reads the novel as a fable of appetite: "Not feeding the King is worse than starving the people." The monarch who "lacks the usual stomach for brutality" faces subjects who are "baby birds, mouths open and trembling for news of their new ruler." She catches the strands bleeding into each other — "the king drops an egg and it miraculously rolls, as if made from rubber" — evidence that the Curator's imagination is authoring the past she claims to recover.

**Conor Truax, Artforum.** The most theoretical reading: this is "'historical fiction' that leans away from mediating history and instead turns toward exploring the apparatuses by which history is mediated." The King's ledger entry is "a discarded sheet taking on the shape of a ghost." Truax's dissent is specific: the Curator's closing speech ("No record-maker should have the last say") is "heavy-handed," and in delivering it "Perry seems to back away, at the very end, from the novel's more daring formal maneuvers."

**Bookishelf (independent).** The strongest long-tail find. It frames the novel through the ethics of representation: the Curator's "whole profession is deciding what a dead stranger had for dinner, and she takes that responsibility more seriously than most historians take theirs." It identifies the book's governing metaphor in the Curator's lecture about a nineteenth-century modelmaker whose wax replicas of 286 Italian apple varieties are now the only proof they existed: the catalogue preserves everything except "flavour. Which is the entire purpose of an apple."

**Jackie Law, Bookmunch/neverimitate.** A close structural reading: the short "nugget" chapters keep pace while enacting partial knowledge (a point sharpened in the comments by Elle Thinks: the chapters are "structural reflections of theme – what we can know is only ever partial"). Law praises what Perry leaves out — "unnecessary detail such as what people looked like is avoided."

## 5. Fault lines

No major legacy outlet pans the book; the disagreements are about the cost of its design. (a) **Withholding as achievement vs. evasion:** Harrison and Ovenden treat the withheld ending as the book's moral core; Truax thinks the ending talks about withholding instead of enacting it ("corralling its enigmatic structure into something ordered"). (b) **Style vs. substance:** Balanescu concedes the novel "sometimes opts for style over substance"; Thomas-Corr's Sunday Times notice (paywalled, via Booker) similarly finds the characters' sensibilities anachronistically modern. (c) **Period anchoring:** Thomas-Corr's "dream or fairytale" complaint is answered by the Bookishelf review, which acknowledges the "deliberately unplaced" court — "no country, no date, no names" — as both the source of its "fable-like clarity" and the reason "you never get your feet under you." (d) **Structure:** Bookishelf warns the opening fifty pages "read as a beautiful notebook"; Law reads the same fragmentation as the book's point.

## 8. Synthesis (the agent's own view — clearly labeled)

The critical split maps almost perfectly onto each reviewer's tolerance for the book's own subject. Everyone agrees the novel is about the impossibility of recovering the past; the reviewers who treat formal frustration as the honest enactment of that impossibility (Harrison, Bookishelf, Law) rate it highest, while those who want the novel to resolve into a statement (Truax on the ending) or a period (Thomas-Corr) rate it lower. The Waterstones win plus Booker shortlisting suggests the prize establishments sided with the first camp. Notably, the deepest engagement this run found came not from legacy outlets but from an independent site (Bookishelf) and an art magazine (Artforum) — the book's museum-world subject seems to have attracted critics who think professionally about curation and display.
