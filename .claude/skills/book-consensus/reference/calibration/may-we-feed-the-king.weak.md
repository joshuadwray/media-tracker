# Critical consensus: *May We Feed the King* by Rebecca Perry (2026)

## 1. Where the book stands

Rebecca Perry’s debut novel was published in the UK by Granta on 29 January 2026, according to the Booker Prizes catalogue: https://thebookerprizes.com/the-booker-library/books/may-we-feed-the-king. The Booker Prize site records it as longlisted for the 2026 prize, and quotes the judges calling its combination of a curator recreating banquets and a reluctant medieval king “a wonderfully enticing” set of themes. Perry won the 2026 Waterstones Debut Fiction Prize from a shortlist of six, an award voted for by Waterstones booksellers: https://www.theguardian.com/books/2026/jul/16/rebecca-perry-waterstones-debut-fiction-prize-may-we-feed-the-king. The Guardian’s prize report identifies her previous books as the poetry collections *Beauty/Beauty* and *Stone Fruit*, and the creative-nonfiction book *On Trampolining*; her poetry had been shortlisted for the TS Eliot Prize. The US publisher Transit Books gives a 29 September 2026 paperback date and labels the novel “Shortlisted for the 2026 Booker Prize,” but the Booker’s own catalogue says “Longlisted”; this report treats the official Booker catalogue as controlling and records the publisher-page discrepancy: https://www.transitbooks.org/books/maywefeedtheking.

## 2. The consensus

Across the fetched criticism, the novel’s strongest consensus is formal and thematic rather than plot-based: Perry uses a dual timeline, fragmentary scenes, replica food and an unnamed curator to make historical reconstruction feel both sensuous and suspect. Melissa Harrison in *The Guardian*, Olivia Ovenden in *The Observer*, Edward Heathman in *The Manchester Review*, Roseanna Pendlebury in *Strange Horizons* and the Booker judges all read the book as an inquiry into how the past is made, preserved and misread. The main reservation, voiced most sharply by Rosemary Goring in *The Herald* and more briefly by Miriam Balanescu in *The Irish Times*, is that the poetic opacity can become repetitive, anachronistic or more philosophical than historical.

## 4. In-depth readings

Melissa Harrison in *The Guardian* says the novel turns its refusal to explain into method. Its precise objects and deliberately unspecified larger circumstances make readers experience the frustration of historical knowledge: we want “clues” and “certainty,” but the book makes us observe our own compulsive pattern-making. https://www.theguardian.com/books/2026/jan/23/may-we-feed-the-king-by-rebecca-perry-review-a-dazzling-puzzle-box-of-a-debut

Olivia Ovenden in *The Observer* goes furthest on the politics of archives. The novel begins as a project of historical accuracy, then asks why records deserve trust and how propaganda enters them. Ovenden’s strongest formulation is that Perry resists explanations and creates a story that can be “blown free of dust, held to the light.” https://observer.co.uk/culture/books/article/a-feast-for-an-unfit-king

Edward Heathman in *The Manchester Review* connects form to power. The curator’s displays, titled chapters and replica food make artifice visible, while the King’s quiet refusal to wage war, collect taxes or perform authority becomes a “quiet boycott.” Heathman reads the resulting moral questions—about rulers, institutions and communities—as “an indictment of our time.” https://www.themanchesterreview.co.uk/?p=13324

Roseanna Pendlebury in *Strange Horizons* treats the book’s two protagonists as inversions of each other. The curator wants immersion and disappearance into another world; the King wants the self that kingship demands he surrender. The novel’s formal disjointedness therefore becomes a way to defend multiplicity and interpretation rather than a problem to be solved. https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/non-fiction/may-we-feed-the-king-by-rebecca-perry/

Conor Truax in *Artforum* emphasizes historical simulacra: the curator’s invented scenes do not merely illustrate the past but expose the machinery by which the past is invented. Truax praises that deconstruction, though says the concluding speech corrals the novel’s more daring ambiguity into a somewhat orderly statement. https://www.artforum.com/columns/rebecca-perry-may-we-feed-the-king-book-review-1234760319/

## 5. Fault lines

The main disagreement is over whether the book’s ahistorical, fairy-tale quality is productive. Goring in *The Herald* finds the King’s modern sensibility anachronistic and says the novel is “more a glittering exercise of the imagination” than an engagement with the past. Balanescu in *The Irish Times* similarly notes that the style can outrun substance. By contrast, Harrison, Ovenden and Pendlebury treat the refusal of period specificity as the book’s central formal argument. A second, narrower fault line concerns the ending: Ovenden and Pendlebury value its open-endedness, while Truax finds the final speech somewhat over-explicit.

## 6. Reader sentiment

No reader-only sentiment is included in the consensus. Search results surfaced Goodreads, StoryGraph and Reddit discussion, but those sources were not used as professional criticism.

## 7. Read-in-full shortlist

- *The Guardian* — the clearest account of the puzzle-box structure and the novel’s theory of historical uncertainty.
- *The Observer* — the strongest mainstream discussion of archives, propaganda and the politics of evidence.
- *The Manchester Review* — the most useful treatment of curatorial artifice, food and institutional power.
- *Strange Horizons* — the fullest reading of the curator and King as formal and psychological inversions.

## 8. Synthesis

The criticism converges on a book that makes uncertainty its subject and its reading experience. The King’s refusal to perform power, and the curator’s attempt to manufacture presence from scraps, are parallel acts of resistance to fixed roles. The praise is strongest when the novel’s fragments force readers to supply connections; the reservations arise when the prose or final explanation tells readers what its ambiguity means. The critical consensus is therefore favorable but conditional: this is an ambitious, sensuous debut for readers willing to treat incompleteness as the point.
